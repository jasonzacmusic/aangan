§ 01 — WHY THIS BOARD

The decision, settled

Both candidate DACs sound excellent. The Raspberry Pi DAC Pro wins on the thing that matters for a rig feeding a studio console.

What makes it right

  • Native balanced/differential output — not RCA wired into an XLR shell. Genuine balanced signal.
  • Official XLR daughter board plugs onto the P7/P9 headers — no Neutriks to buy, nothing to solder.
  • 0–4V RMS balanced vs 2V on RCA — a stronger, cleaner feed to your desk.
  • First-party Raspberry Pi (rebranded IQaudio) — best driver and documentation support.
  • 24-bit / 192kHz PCM5242 with a headphone amp for monitoring.
  • RCA out in parallel — you keep a second output for free.

What it removes from the build

  • NOT NEEDED  2× Neutrik NC3MD connectors
  • NOT NEEDED  Project box to mount them in
  • NOT NEEDED  Soldering iron, solder, heat-shrink
  • NOT NEEDED  RCA-to-bare wiring pair
  • NOT NEEDED  Drilling anything
  • Fewer parts, fewer failure points, better signal.
Honest note on the chip comparison: the InnoMaker's ES9038Q2M is, on paper, a more prestigious converter than the PCM5242. For a pure hi-fi listening rig that argument would carry weight. For a stage instrument feeding a desk over cable, a true balanced output path matters more than the converter chip — balanced rejects hum and interference in a way no amount of DAC quality compensates for. That's the whole reasoning.
§ 02 — THE SIGNAL CHAIN

How sound travels

Keys → Pianoteq models the piano → PCM5242 converts → XLR board → balanced out to console
What's actually happening: your keyboard sends only note information — which key, how hard, no sound at all. Pianoteq calculates what a real piano would sound like from the physics of strings, hammers and soundboard. The DAC turns that into an electrical signal. The XLR board sends it out balanced. That's why a board smaller than your hand can sound like a concert grand.

§ 04 — BEFORE YOU ORDER

Four things to check first

Five minutes of checking here prevents the two things that could stall this build.

Is the XLR board in stock in India?

This is the one genuine unknown. Call or message Robu.in and ask specifically for the XLR board for the Raspberry Pi DAC Pro. If they don't stock it, ask whether they can order it in, and how long.

If it can't be sourced quickly: buy the DAC Pro anyway and feed its RCA output into a passive stereo DI box. You still get first-party support and true balanced XLR out of the DI. That fallback is genuinely good.

Do the P7/P9 headers come pre-soldered?

Newer revisions of the DAC Pro ship with bare pads instead of a pre-fitted 6-pin female header on P7/P9. If yours arrives that way, the XLR board needs a header soldered on — a small job, but you'd want to know beforehand.

Ask the seller which revision they're shipping. If it's the bare-pad version, add a 6-pin female header to the order and plan five minutes of soldering.

Which USB port does your keyboard have?

Look at the socket on your MIDI keyboard. Square-ish with bevelled corners = USB-B (most common). Small oval = USB-C. Five-pin round = DIN MIDI, which needs a USB-MIDI interface instead.

Order the matching cable. This is a ₹200 part that can block the whole build if wrong.

Don't buy a closed case yet

The XLR board sits above the Pi's USB and Ethernet ports — a standard Pi 5 case will not close over it. Options: an open-frame/stackable mount with the official Active Cooler, a taller case that clears the daughter board, or mount the whole stack inside the transport case.

Wait until the boards are in hand, measure, then choose. It's the one part where guessing wastes money.

§ 05 — WHEN IT ARRIVES

Check before you build

  • DAC Pro is genuine — Raspberry Pi branding, clean board finish, no bent pins on the GPIO connector
  • P7/P9 headers — confirm whether they're pre-soldered or bare pads (determines your next step)
  • XLR board — sockets clean and undamaged, connector matches the DAC Pro's header spacing
  • Pi 5 — no damage, GPIO pins straight
  • microSD — genuine (counterfeits are common; buy from reputable sellers)
  • PSU — official 27W, correct plug type for India
  • USB-C cables — confirm they're rated 5A/100W, not generic charging cables
  • Keyboard cable — matches the socket you checked in §04
  • Test-fit before permanent mounting — stack the boards loosely and confirm everything physically clears
§ 06 — PHYSICAL ASSEMBLY

Building the stack

Power off, work on a clean surface

Pi unplugged, no power anywhere. Static-safe surface if you have one. Have the transport case nearby to check fit as you go.

2 min

Fit the Active Cooler (if using)

If you're using the official Pi 5 Active Cooler, fit it before the DAC — it clips onto the Pi and connects to the fan header. Fitting it later means dismantling the stack.

5 min

Seat the DAC Pro on the GPIO

Line up the DAC Pro's connector with all 40 GPIO pins and press down evenly. It should seat fully with no gap and no pin visible. Use standoffs if supplied so the board sits rigid rather than flexing.

Check: the P7/P9 headers should now be sitting above the Pi's USB/Ethernet ports.

5 min

Solder the 6-pin header (only if needed)

If your DAC Pro shipped with bare P7/P9 pads, solder a 6-pin female header onto them now. Six joints, low difficulty — but do it before the XLR board goes on.

Skip entirely if the headers came pre-fitted.

10 min

Fit the XLR daughter board

Plug the XLR board onto the P7/P9 headers. It sits above the USB/Ethernet ports with the XLR sockets facing outward. Confirm it's fully seated and the sockets are accessible.

5 min

Connect the touchscreen

Official display: connect the DSI ribbon to the Pi's display port, note the ribbon orientation. Generic HDMI touchscreen: HDMI cable for video plus a USB cable for touch input.

10 min

Mount the whole stack

Fix the assembly into your chosen mounting — open frame, tall case, or directly into the foam of the transport case. Everything should be rigid; nothing should move when the rig is picked up.

Tidy any cable with velcro ties. Label the XLR sockets L and R.

30 min
The rule for this whole section: nothing gets powered until the stack is fully seated and checked. A half-seated HAT powered up is the one way to damage a board.

§ 08 — FLASHING THE OS

Getting the Pi running

Install Raspberry Pi Imager on your laptop

Download from raspberrypi.com. Works on Windows, Mac and Linux.

5 min

Choose Raspberry Pi OS (64-bit)

Pick the standard Raspberry Pi OS (64-bit) with desktop — you need a GUI for Pianoteq's interface on the touchscreen.

Not Home Assistant OS — that's for the house-automation Pi. Different machine, different OS.

2 min

Pre-configure in Imager settings

Before writing, open the gear/settings icon and set: hostname (e.g. pianopi), username and password, Wi-Fi network and password, locale/timezone, and enable SSH.

This saves you needing a keyboard and monitor for first boot — you can SSH straight in.

5 min

Write to the microSD

Insert the card, select it, and write. Takes several minutes. Let it verify.

10 min

First boot

Insert the card into the Pi, connect the touchscreen and power. First boot takes a couple of minutes. Confirm the desktop appears on the touchscreen and touch input responds.

10 min

Update everything

# bring the system fully up to date
sudo apt update
sudo apt full-upgrade -y
sudo reboot

Do this before installing anything else — it prevents most driver oddities.

20 min
§ 09 — ENABLING THE DAC

Making the Pi use the DAC Pro

Edit the boot config

sudo nano /boot/firmware/config.txt

On older OS versions this file is at /boot/config.txt — check both.

2 min

Disable onboard audio, enable the DAC overlay

# find this line and set it to off
dtparam=audio=off

# comment out the automatic audio overlay if present
#dtoverlay=vc4-kms-v3d   ← leave graphics alone, only audio matters

# add the DAC Pro overlay
dtoverlay=iqaudio-dacplus

Verify the exact overlay name against Raspberry Pi's current audio documentation before relying on it — overlay names occasionally change between OS releases, and the DAC Pro historically uses the IQaudio overlay family.

5 min

Reboot and verify the DAC is seen

sudo reboot

# after reboot — list audio playback devices
aplay -l

You should see the DAC listed as a sound card. If you only see HDMI, the overlay isn't loading — recheck the config line.

5 min

Set the DAC as default output

Use the desktop's audio settings (right-click the speaker icon) or raspi-config to select the DAC as the default audio device.

3 min

Test audio BEFORE installing Pianoteq

# plays a test tone through the default device
speaker-test -c2 -twav

Connect the XLR outputs to your console and confirm you hear the test tone on both channels. Prove the audio path works before adding Pianoteq — it isolates any problem to one thing at a time.

10 min
This is the step most likely to need current documentation. Overlay names and config file paths shift between OS releases. If aplay -l doesn't show the DAC, check Raspberry Pi's official audio boards documentation for the current overlay name rather than guessing — or hand this section to Claude Code and let it fetch the current instructions.
§ 10 — INSTALLING PIANOTEQ PRO

The instrument itself

Log into your Modartt account

Go to modartt.com and sign in with the account holding your Pianoteq Pro licence. Your Pro licence covers the ARM/Raspberry Pi build.

2 min

Download the ARM / Raspberry Pi build

In the downloads area, find the Linux ARM version — this is the one for Raspberry Pi. Not the x86 Linux build.

Download directly on the Pi if it has a browser, or download on your laptop and transfer via USB drive or scp.

10 min

Unpack and run

# extract the archive (name will vary by version)
cd ~/Downloads
unzip pianoteq_linux_arm*.zip
cd Pianoteq*/arm-64bit/
./Pianoteq*

Pianoteq runs directly from the extracted folder — there's typically no system-wide install step.

10 min

Activate your licence

On first run, Pianoteq will ask for activation. Enter your Modartt credentials or licence key. It needs internet for this step — after activation it runs offline, which is what you want on stage.

10 min

Select the audio device

In Pianoteq's Options → Devices, set the output device to the DAC. Set the sample rate to 48000 Hz to start.

Also confirm your MIDI keyboard appears in the MIDI input list and shows activity when you press keys.

10 min

Play something

Press a key. You should hear piano through the console. This is the moment the rig becomes an instrument.

1 min
Version caveat, honestly: the exact package name, folder structure and activation flow change between Pianoteq releases. The commands above show the shape of the job — follow Modartt's current instructions for your downloaded version. If anything differs, trust their documentation over this page.
§ 11 — TUNING LATENCY

Making it feel like a real piano

This is the step that separates "it makes sound" from "it feels like an instrument." Take your time here.

Understand the trade-off

Smaller buffer = less delay between pressing a key and hearing sound, but more risk of clicks and dropouts if the CPU can't keep up.

Larger buffer = rock solid, but you feel a lag that makes playing unnatural.

Your goal: the smallest buffer that never clicks.

Start safe, then reduce

In Options → Devices, start at a comfortable buffer (e.g. 512 samples). Play. Then step down — 256, then 128 — testing thoroughly at each level.

20 min

Stress-test at each setting

Don't just play single notes. Play dense chords, fast passages, and heavy sustain pedal — that's when the CPU works hardest. If you hear a single click, go back up one level.

15 min

Reduce polyphony if needed

If you can't get low latency without clicks, lower Pianoteq's polyphony setting (max simultaneous notes). Going from 256 to 128 voices frees significant CPU and is rarely audible in normal playing.

10 min

Close everything else

Nothing else should run on this Pi. No browser, no background updates, no other software. The whole point of a dedicated machine is that Pianoteq gets the entire CPU.

5 min

Long-session test

Once tuned, play for 30 minutes continuously. Thermal throttling and slow memory issues only show up over time. If it's clean after 30 minutes, it's clean.

30 min
What "good" feels like: when you press a key, the sound should feel simultaneous with your finger — no perceptible gap. If you're consciously aware of a delay, keep tuning. A well-tuned Pi 5 running Pianoteq is genuinely responsive enough for serious playing.
§ 12 — AUTO-START & BACKUP

Making it a real appliance

Auto-start Pianoteq on boot

Create a desktop autostart entry so Pianoteq launches full-screen when the Pi powers on:

mkdir -p ~/.config/autostart
nano ~/.config/autostart/pianoteq.desktop
[Desktop Entry]
Type=Application
Name=Pianoteq
Exec=/home/pi/Pianoteq/arm-64bit/Pianoteq --fullscreen
X-GNOME-Autostart-enabled=true

Adjust the Exec path to match where you extracted Pianoteq.

15 min

Disable screen blanking

You don't want the touchscreen going dark mid-performance. Disable screen blanking in raspi-config under Display Options.

5 min

Test the full cold-start

Power off completely. Power on. The rig should reach a playable Pianoteq with no keyboard, no mouse, no intervention. That's the target.

5 min

Back up the microSD

With everything working, image the card to your laptop (Raspberry Pi Imager, Win32DiskImager, or dd). Store it somewhere safe.

Why this matters: if the card fails during a tour or session, you restore in fifteen minutes instead of rebuilding for a day.

20 min

Optional: a spare card

Write the same image to a second microSD and keep it in the transport case. Card failure becomes a 60-second swap.

15 min
§ 13 — STAGE SETUP

Taking it out

In the transport case

  • The rig, mounted rigid in foam
  • Power bank, charged
  • 27W PSU as backup if mains is available
  • 2× XLR cables
  • Keyboard USB cable
  • Spare microSD with the backup image

Setup routine on site

  • Place rig, connect keyboard USB
  • Connect XLR L and R to the desk or DI
  • Connect power (bank or mains)
  • Power on — it boots straight into Pianoteq
  • Ask for level check on both channels
  • Play a few bars to confirm feel and latency
Label everything. On a dark stage under time pressure, clear labels on the XLR sockets (L / R), the power input, and the keyboard USB turn setup into a thirty-second job. Cheap tape and a marker; enormously worth it.
On fan noise: active cooling makes some noise. It's load-dependent and quiet in normal use, fine for stage and most studio work. If you're ever recording in a dead-silent room with the rig beside a microphone, move the rig further away or lower the fan curve for that session. Small trade for the reliability cooling gives on long sessions.
§ 14 — THE TEST CHECKLIST

Done when every box ticks

  • DAC recognised — aplay -l shows the DAC as a sound card
  • Test tone plays on both channels through the XLR outputs before Pianoteq is involved
  • Pianoteq activated with your Pro licence and launches cleanly
  • Keyboard detected — pressing a key produces sound
  • Both channels correct — Left arrives on left, Right on right, at the console
  • No hum or buzz with nothing playing — a quiet noise floor
  • Balanced level is healthy — the console sees a strong signal without needing extreme gain
  • Latency feels immediate — no perceptible gap between key and sound
  • No clicks under load — dense chords and heavy pedal for five minutes straight
  • 30-minute session clean — no thermal throttling, no degradation
  • Touchscreen works — you can change instruments and settings by touch
  • Auto-start verified — cold power-on reaches playable Pianoteq unaided
  • Power bank tested — full rig runs on battery with no under-voltage warning
  • Screen doesn't blank during a long idle period
  • microSD backed up and ideally a spare card written
  • Everything labelled — XLR L/R, power, MIDI
When all sixteen tick, you have a proven, gig-ready world-class piano — and you've validated the Raspberry Pi approach before spending on the second Pi for the house automation.

§ 16 — AFTER IT WORKS

What comes next

Use it on a real session

A bench test isn't the real test. Play the rig in an actual recording or rehearsal and see how it holds up under real conditions.

Buy the second Pi for the house

With the Pi approach proven and your team now familiar with the ecosystem, order the House Pi for the automation build. It'll go faster because you've done this once.

Link the two machines

The Piano Pi and House Pi talk over the network. The house app can show piano rig status and send simple cues — while staying decoupled so house alerts can never touch the piano's real-time audio thread.

Explore Pianoteq's instrument packs

Your Pro licence covers the full instrument range — grands, uprights, electric pianos, harpsichords, vibraphones. Each is tiny to install because they're models, not sample libraries.

Why this was the right first project: the piano rig is your fastest complete win. It validates the hardware, the software approach, and your team's confidence with Raspberry Pi — all before you commit to the much larger house-automation build.
